My name is Meenakshi Ammal, and I have been weaving and dyeing Sungudi sarees in Madurai for over forty years. My mother taught me, and her mother taught her. Let me tell you how I make a Sungudi.

First, I begin with the silk. It must be pure Madurai silk — fine and smooth, almost like cool water running through your fingers. Before I do anything else, I wash it in warm water. Not hot — if the water is too hot, the silk becomes dull and loses its natural shine. I know the temperature is right when I can keep my hand in the basin without discomfort. I gently squeeze the fabric from end to end, never twisting, until it is soaked all the way through. When I lift it, it should feel evenly heavy — no dry patches, no part that is wetter than another. This evenness is everything. If the silk dries unevenly before I begin tying, the dye will absorb differently in different places, and the pattern will look careless.

Once the silk is properly damp, I spread it flat on my work surface. I take my chalk and begin marking the tying points. For a traditional Sungudi pattern, I work in a grid — rows and columns, each mark about one and a half centimetres apart. The spacing decides how fine or bold the pattern will be. I use a very light touch with the chalk. Those marks must wash out completely at the end — if I press too hard, the chalk leaves a trace or, worse, weakens the silk at that point.

Now comes the tying — this is the real heart of Sungudi. This is where the craft lives in my hands.

I pick up the fabric at the first marked point between my right thumb and index finger, pulling up a tiny peak — just three or four millimetres. With my left hand, I wrap the cotton thread around the base of that peak. One wrap, two wraps, three wraps — each one pulled tight. I can feel the resistance in my fingertips when the thread is secure. It is a small, firm tension — not so tight that the silk is damaged, but tight enough that no dye will pass through. After the third wrap, I tie a double knot and cut the thread close. Then I move to the next mark, and the next. My fingers know the motion by feel now. After so many years, I do not think about each knot — my hands simply do it.

On a good day I can tie five hundred knots in one hour. On a bad day, when my fingers are tired, maybe three hundred. Younger artisans often ask me how long it takes to get this speed. I always tell them: do not think about speed. Think only about consistency. Every circle must be the same size. If one knot is loose, that one circle will be grey instead of white, and the pattern breaks. It is better to tie slowly and evenly than to rush and ruin the piece.

After tying — and a full saree can have two thousand knots or more — I prepare the dye bath. I heat the natural dye in a large vessel. The colour I use most is a deep crimson made from pomegranate rind. I heat the bath until it is hot but not boiling — about the heat you would use for a strong cup of tea. I lower the tied fabric in slowly, making sure every part is submerged. I stir it gently with a wooden stick every few minutes. After twenty minutes, I test a corner by pressing it against a white cloth. The colour should transfer richly. If it looks pale, I give it five more minutes.

When the colour is right, I remove the fabric and let it cool in clear water. Then, when it is cool enough to handle, I begin to untie.

This is my favourite moment. I take small scissors and cut each knot — snip, snip, snip. There is a soft sound when the thread releases — a tiny snap — and I know each one will reveal a clean white circle. As the thread falls away, the pattern emerges slowly, dot by dot. The crimson and white, the geometric grid — it is like watching the fabric remember what it was before the dye. When I hold the finished saree up to the light and see that the circles are even, all the same size, properly spaced — that is the feeling I have been working toward since I was a girl standing at my mother's side.

We wear Sungudi at weddings and temple festivals. The red is auspicious — it means prosperity and blessing. When a bride wears a Sungudi saree from our family, she carries something much older than any one person. She carries the memory of every woman who tied these knots before her.
